Lesson 1 of 6

Forces and Free-Body Diagrams

You should be able to…

  • Identify the object or system you are analyzing.
  • Draw an FBD with every force labeled by type and the object exerting it.
  • Tell internal forces from external ones.
Big idea: Forces come from interactions. Dynamics questions usually reward correct representations and reasoning before final numbers. A strong FBD is often half the solution.
Common mistake: Do not invent a “force of motion.” If an object is moving right, that does not automatically mean there is a rightward force.

Pre-Check: Catch the Misconceptions

Ungraded pre-check: answer before you start. You will retake these same questions in the Unit Review to see your growth.

Question 1: Constant Velocity

A box slides to the right at constant velocity. What must be true about the net force?

Explanation

Constant velocity means acceleration is zero. Since $\Sigma F = ma$, the net force must be zero.

Question 2: Normal Force

A box rests on a horizontal table with no vertical acceleration. Which statement is true?

Explanation

The normal force is a contact force. On a level surface with no other vertical forces and no vertical acceleration, $F_N=mg$.

Question 3: Action-Reaction Pair

A student pushes a wall. The wall pushes back on the student with...

Explanation

Newton's third law pairs are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, and act on different objects.

Dynamics Roadmap

1. Identify the Object

Only draw forces acting on the object you are analyzing. Do not draw forces the object exerts on other things.

2. Draw the FBD

Represent every force as an arrow starting from the object. Label force types clearly.

3. Choose Axes

Use horizontal/vertical for flat surfaces and rotated axes for ramps.

4. Apply $\Sigma F = ma$

Use Newton's second law separately in each direction.

AP Exam Mindset: Dynamics questions usually reward correct representations and reasoning before final numbers. A strong FBD is often half the solution.

Systems: Internal vs. External Forces

Choose the System

Decide what you are analyzing: one object, or several objects treated together. Only draw forces acting on the object or system you chose.

Internal Forces Cancel

When objects are treated as one system, the forces between them (such as the contact force between two pushed blocks, or the rope tension between connected blocks) are internal and cancel. Only external forces change the motion of the system.

Force Types You Must Recognize

Weight$$F_g=mg$$Gravity's force on an object. Direction: down toward Earth.
Normal Force$$F_N$$Contact force perpendicular to a surface. Not always equal to $mg$.
Friction$$f_k=\mu_k F_N$$Kinetic friction opposes sliding motion.
Static Friction$$f_s\leq \mu_s F_N$$Static friction adjusts up to a maximum value.
Tension$$F_T$$Pulling force from a rope or string along the rope.
Spring Force$$F_s=kx$$Restoring force from a stretched or compressed spring.
Common trap: Do not invent a “force of motion.” If an object is moving right, that does not automatically mean there is a rightward force.

Level 1 • Force Identification

Problem 1: Book on a Table

A book rests on a table. It is not moving.

  1. Draw or describe the forces on the book.
  2. What is the net force?
  3. Does the book exert a force on the table?
Reveal solution

Forces on book: weight downward and normal force upward. Since acceleration is zero, $F_N=F_g$ and net force is zero. The book exerts a downward contact force on the table; the table exerts an upward normal force on the book.

Quick Check

AP Paragraph

A block is pulled to the right across a rough horizontal floor and accelerates to the right. A student adds a “force of motion” pointing right to the free-body diagram.

Write a short paragraph that agrees or disagrees with including it.

Reveal strong response

Disagree. The correct forces are weight down, normal up, applied force right, and friction left. There is no separate “force of motion.” Motion describes what the object is doing; force describes interactions. Every arrow on an FBD needs an object that exerts it, and nothing exerts a “force of motion.”

More Paragraph Practice

AP Paragraph

A book rests on a table. A student says, “The two forces on the book are its weight and the force the book exerts on the table.”

In a short paragraph, explain what’s wrong with the student’s FBD and draw the correct one.

Reveal model answer

An FBD shows only forces exerted on the book. The force the book exerts on the table acts on the table, so it doesn’t belong on the book’s FBD. The correct forces on the book are Earth’s gravitational force (weight) pointing down and the table’s normal force pointing up. They’re equal in magnitude because the book’s acceleration is zero, but they aren’t a Newton’s 3rd law pair, because they act on the same object and come from different interactions.

Lesson 2 of 6

Newton's Laws and Net Force

You should be able to…

  • Connect net force to acceleration, not velocity.
  • Apply the 2nd law to one object and to a system.
  • Identify Newton's 3rd law pairs and explain why they never cancel.
Big idea: The net force determines the acceleration. If the net force is zero, velocity stays constant (including staying at rest). Forces come in interaction pairs: A on B and B on A.
Common mistake: Third-law forces do not cancel because they act on different objects.

Newton's Laws: Student-Friendly Meaning

Newton's 1st Law

If net force is zero, velocity stays constant. That includes staying at rest.

No net force does not mean no motion. It means no change in motion.

Newton's 2nd Law

The net force determines the acceleration.

$$\Sigma \vec{F}=m\vec{a}$$

Acceleration points in the direction of the net force.

Newton's 3rd Law

Forces come in interaction pairs: A on B and B on A.

Third-law forces do not cancel because they act on different objects.

Level 2 • Net Force

Problem 2: Horizontal Push

A $6.0\,\text{kg}$ cart is pushed right with $36\,\text{N}$. Friction is negligible.

  1. What is the net force?
  2. What is the acceleration?
Reveal solution

$\Sigma F=36\,\text{N}$ right. $a=\Sigma F/m=36/6=6.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ right.

Level 8 • Elevator / Apparent Weight

Problem 8: Scale in an Elevator

A $60\,\text{kg}$ student stands on a scale in an elevator accelerating upward at $2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$.

  1. Draw the FBD.
  2. What does the scale read?
Reveal solution

Forces: normal force up, weight down. Upward is positive. $F_N-mg=ma$. $F_N=m(g+a)=60(10+2)=720\,\text{N}$. The scale reads $720\,\text{N}$.

Level 17 • Systems and Internal Forces

Problem 17: Pushing Two Blocks

Two blocks touch on a frictionless floor: a $3.0\,\text{kg}$ block on the left and a $2.0\,\text{kg}$ block on the right. A $20\,\text{N}$ horizontal push is applied to the left side of the $3.0\,\text{kg}$ block.

  1. Find the acceleration of the blocks.
  2. Find the force the $3.0\,\text{kg}$ block exerts on the $2.0\,\text{kg}$ block.
  3. The same $20\,\text{N}$ push is applied to the right side of the $2.0\,\text{kg}$ block instead. Does the contact force between the blocks change? Explain.
Reveal scaffold

Use the whole system for the acceleration, since the contact forces are internal and cancel. Then draw the FBD of only the block that's being pushed by the other one.

Reveal solution

$a=20/5=4.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. The only horizontal force on the $2.0\,\text{kg}$ block is the contact force, so $F=(2)(4)=8.0\,\text{N}$. Pushing from the other side, the contact force now has to accelerate the $3.0\,\text{kg}$ block: $F=(3)(4)=12\,\text{N}$. It changes because the contact force accelerates whichever block isn't being pushed directly.

Level 22 • Newton's 3rd Law and Gravity

Problem 22: Earth and Moon

Earth's mass is about 81 times the Moon's mass.

  1. Compare the force Earth exerts on the Moon with the force the Moon exerts on Earth.
  2. Compare the accelerations those forces cause.
Reveal solution

The forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, a Newton's 3rd law pair. The accelerations aren't equal. Since $a=F/m$, the Moon's acceleration is 81 times Earth's.

Quick Check

AP Paragraph

Paragraph Response Prompt

A student says: “If an object is moving to the right, there must be a net force to the right.”

Write a paragraph that agrees or disagrees. Use Newton's laws and an example.

Reveal strong response

The statement is not always correct. Newton's second law says that net force determines acceleration, not velocity. An object moving to the right at constant velocity has zero acceleration, so the net force on it is zero. A net force to the right would mean the object is accelerating to the right, so its rightward velocity would be increasing or its leftward velocity would be decreasing.

Lesson 3 of 6

Friction and Springs

You should be able to…

  • Decide whether friction is static or kinetic.
  • Use the fact that static friction is at most $\mu_sF_N$.
  • Use $F=kx$ with the direction toward equilibrium.
Big idea: Static friction adjusts as needed, up to a maximum value; once something slides, kinetic friction $f_k=\mu_kF_N$ opposes the sliding. A spring exerts a restoring force that grows with the stretch or compression.
Common mistake: $f_s$ is not automatically equal to $\mu_sF_N$. That is only the maximum possible value.

Friction: Static vs. Kinetic

Friction Decision Tree

  1. Is it sliding? Use $f_k=\mu_k F_N$.
  2. Is it not sliding? Static friction adjusts as needed.
  3. Maximum static friction: $f_{s,max}=\mu_s F_N$.
  4. If needed force exceeds max static friction: it starts sliding.
Major AP trap: $f_s$ is not automatically equal to $\mu_sF_N$. That is only the maximum possible value.

Static Friction Tester

Springs: Hooke's Law

Spring Force$$F_s=kx$$Restoring force from a stretched or compressed spring.

Direction Matters

The spring force points toward equilibrium. The stretch is proportional to the force. At equilibrium $x=0$, so the spring force (and the acceleration it causes) is zero.

Level 3 • Friction Included

Problem 3: Push with Friction

A $10\,\text{kg}$ box is pushed right with $70\,\text{N}$ across a floor. Kinetic friction is $25\,\text{N}$ left.

  1. Draw the FBD.
  2. Calculate the net force.
  3. Calculate the acceleration.
Reveal solution

Horizontal forces: applied force right and friction left. Vertical forces balance. $\Sigma F_x=70-25=45\,\text{N}$. $a=45/10=4.5\,\text{m/s}^2$ right.

Level 4 • Kinetic Friction

Problem 4: Coefficient of Friction

A $5.0\,\text{kg}$ block slides across a horizontal surface with $\mu_k=0.30$. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$.

  1. Find the normal force.
  2. Find the kinetic friction force.
  3. If no other horizontal force acts, what is the acceleration?
Reveal solution

$F_N=mg=(5)(10)=50\,\text{N}$. $f_k=\mu_kF_N=(0.30)(50)=15\,\text{N}$. If friction is the only horizontal force, $a=15/5=3.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ opposite the motion.

Level 5 • Static Friction

Problem 5: Will It Move?

A $20\,\text{kg}$ crate rests on the floor. The coefficient of static friction is $0.50$. A student pushes horizontally with $80\,\text{N}$.

  1. Find $f_{s,max}$.
  2. Does the crate move?
  3. What is the actual static friction force?
Reveal solution

$F_N=mg=(20)(10)=200\,\text{N}$. $f_{s,max}=\mu_sF_N=(0.50)(200)=100\,\text{N}$. Since the applied force is only $80\,\text{N}$, the crate does not move. Actual static friction is $80\,\text{N}$ opposite the push.

Level 18 • Spring Force

Problem 18: Hanging Spring

A $2.0\,\text{kg}$ mass hangs at rest from a vertical spring with spring constant $k=200\,\text{N/m}$. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$.

  1. Draw the FBD of the mass.
  2. How far is the spring stretched?
  3. If the mass were doubled, how would the stretch change?
Reveal solution

At rest, $kx=mg$, so $x=\dfrac{(2)(10)}{200}=0.10\,\text{m}$. The stretch is proportional to the force, so doubling the mass doubles the stretch to $0.20\,\text{m}$.

Level 19 • Spring Force and Acceleration

Problem 19: Released Spring

A $0.50\,\text{kg}$ block on a frictionless horizontal surface is attached to a spring with $k=80\,\text{N/m}$. The block is pulled $0.050\,\text{m}$ to the right of equilibrium and released.

  1. Find the magnitude and direction of the spring force just after release.
  2. Find the block's acceleration at that moment.
  3. What is the block's acceleration as it passes through equilibrium? Explain.
Reveal solution

$F=kx=(80)(0.050)=4.0\,\text{N}$ to the left, toward equilibrium. $a=4.0/0.50=8.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ to the left. At equilibrium $x=0$, so the spring force and the acceleration are zero, even though the block is moving at its fastest there.

Quick Check

AP Paragraph

A student pushes horizontally on a heavy crate, but it does not move. A classmate says the static friction force on the crate must equal $\mu_sF_N$.

Write a short paragraph that agrees or disagrees. Use the friction decision tree.

Reveal strong response

Disagree. $f_s$ is not automatically equal to $\mu_sF_N$; that is only the maximum possible value, $f_{s,max}=\mu_sF_N$. The crate is not sliding, so static friction adjusts as needed: it is equal to the push and opposite in direction, so the net force is zero. Only if the needed force exceeds $f_{s,max}$ does the crate start sliding, and then friction becomes kinetic, $f_k=\mu_kF_N$.

More Paragraph Practice

AP Paragraph

A student pushes on a heavy crate with a gradually increasing force, but the crate doesn’t move. A classmate says, “The friction force is always $\mu_sF_N$.”

In a short paragraph, explain why that’s wrong and describe how the friction force changes as the push increases.

Reveal model answer

$\mu_sF_N$ is the maximum static friction, not its value at all times. While the crate stays at rest, its acceleration is zero, so static friction exactly matches the push and grows as the push grows. Once the push passes $\mu_sF_N$, static friction can’t keep up, the crate starts sliding, and kinetic friction $\mu_kF_N$ takes over. That’s usually a bit smaller, so the crate speeds up.

Inclines

  • Explain why $F_N=mg\cos\theta$.

Big idea: For ramps, rotate axes so one axis is parallel to the surface and one axis is perpendicular. Then gravity splits into $mg\sin\theta$ along the ramp and $mg\cos\theta$ into the ramp.

Level 6 • Inclined Plane

Problem 6: Frictionless Ramp

A $4.0\,\text{kg}$ block slides down a frictionless $30^\circ$ incline. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$.

  1. Find the component of gravity parallel to the ramp.
  2. Find the acceleration down the ramp.
  3. Find the normal force.
Reveal solution

$F_{g,\parallel}=mg\sin\theta=(4)(10)(0.5)=20\,\text{N}$. $a=F_{net}/m=20/4=5.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. $F_N=mg\cos30^\circ=(4)(10)(0.866)=34.6\,\text{N}$.

Level 9 • Incline with Friction

Problem 9: Sliding Down a Rough Ramp

A $5.0\,\text{kg}$ box slides down a $37^\circ$ incline. The coefficient of kinetic friction is $\mu_k=0.25$. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$, $\sin37^\circ=0.60$, and $\cos37^\circ=0.80$.

  1. Draw the FBD with axes parallel and perpendicular to the ramp.
  2. Find the normal force and the friction force.
  3. Find the box's acceleration.
Reveal scaffold

The normal force is not $mg$ on a ramp. Nothing accelerates perpendicular to the surface, so $F_N=mg\cos\theta$. Friction points up the ramp because the box slides down.

Reveal solution

$F_N=mg\cos37^\circ=(5)(10)(0.80)=40\,\text{N}$. $f_k=\mu_kF_N=(0.25)(40)=10\,\text{N}$. $F_{g,\parallel}=mg\sin37^\circ=30\,\text{N}$. $a=(30-10)/5=4.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ down the ramp.

Level 10 • Static vs. Kinetic on a Ramp

Problem 10: Will It Slide?

A $2.0\,\text{kg}$ block is placed at rest on a $30^\circ$ incline. The coefficients of friction are $\mu_s=0.40$ and $\mu_k=0.30$. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$.

  1. Find the maximum static friction the ramp can provide.
  2. Does the block stay at rest? Justify with a force comparison.
  3. If it slides, find its acceleration.
Reveal scaffold

Compare the force trying to move the block, $mg\sin\theta$, with the most static friction can do, $\mu_sF_N$. Once it slides, switch to $\mu_k$.

Reveal solution

$F_N=(2)(10)(0.866)=17.3\,\text{N}$, so $f_{s,max}=(0.40)(17.3)=6.9\,\text{N}$. $F_{g,\parallel}=(2)(10)(0.5)=10\,\text{N}$. Since $10\,\text{N}>6.9\,\text{N}$, static friction can't hold it, and it slides. $f_k=(0.30)(17.3)=5.2\,\text{N}$, so $a=(10-5.2)/2=2.4\,\text{m/s}^2$ down the ramp.

Level 11 • Constant Velocity on a Ramp

Problem 11: Pushing a Crate Up a Ramp

A worker pushes a $10\,\text{kg}$ crate up a $37^\circ$ ramp at constant speed, pushing parallel to the ramp. The coefficient of kinetic friction is $0.20$. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$, $\sin37^\circ=0.60$, and $\cos37^\circ=0.80$.

  1. Which way does friction point? Why?
  2. What is the net force on the crate?
  3. How hard must the worker push?
Reveal scaffold

Constant velocity means $\Sigma F=0$. Friction opposes the motion, so it points down the ramp, on the same side as the gravity component.

Reveal solution

Friction points down the ramp, opposite the motion. Net force is zero. $F_N=(10)(10)(0.80)=80\,\text{N}$, so $f_k=(0.20)(80)=16\,\text{N}$. $F_{g,\parallel}=(10)(10)(0.60)=60\,\text{N}$. $F_{push}=60+16=76\,\text{N}$.

Quick Check

AP Paragraph

A student says the normal force on a block resting on a ramp is $mg$, just like on a level table.

Write a short paragraph that agrees or disagrees.

Reveal strong response

Disagree. The normal force is not $mg$ on a ramp. With axes parallel and perpendicular to the surface, nothing accelerates perpendicular to the surface, so the perpendicular forces balance and $F_N=mg\cos\theta$, which is less than $mg$ for any tilted ramp. Only on a level surface with no other vertical forces and no vertical acceleration does $F_N=mg$.

More Paragraph Practice

AP Paragraph

The same block is placed on two frictionless ramps, one at $20^\circ$ and one at $40^\circ$.

Without calculating, compare the normal force and the acceleration on the two ramps, and justify both comparisons in a short paragraph.

Reveal model answer

On the steeper ramp the normal force is smaller and the acceleration is larger. There’s no acceleration perpendicular to the ramp, so $F_N=mg\cos\theta$, and $\cos\theta$ decreases as the angle increases. Along the ramp, the only force is $mg\sin\theta$, so $a=g\sin\theta$, which increases with the angle. Mass doesn’t affect the acceleration on either ramp.

Lesson 5 of 6

Connected Objects and Pulleys

You should be able to…

  • Find acceleration by treating connected objects as one system.
  • Find tension from one object's FBD.
  • Predict direction before calculating.
Big idea: Treat connected objects as one system first: the unbalanced external force accelerates the total mass. Then look at one object by itself to find the tension, and check with the other.
Common mistake: Tension isn't equal to the hanging weight when things accelerate.

System-First Strategy

Step by Step

  1. Predict the direction. Compare the forces pulling each way (for example, the hanging weight versus friction or $m_1g\sin\theta$). Whichever is bigger wins.
  2. Treat both masses together first. The unbalanced external force accelerates the total mass: $a=\Sigma F_{ext}/m_{total}$.
  3. Use one object's FBD to find the tension $T$.
  4. Check with the other object. Both FBDs must give the same $T$.

Why It Works

The rope tension between connected objects is an internal force for the system, so it cancels when you treat them together. To find it, you have to look at one object by itself.

Level 7 • Connected Objects

Problem 7: Two Blocks and a Rope

A $3.0\,\text{kg}$ block on a frictionless table is connected over a pulley to a hanging $2.0\,\text{kg}$ mass. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$.

  1. What external force accelerates the two-block system?
  2. What is the acceleration of the system?
  3. What is the tension in the rope?
Reveal scaffold

Treat both masses together first. The hanging weight is the unbalanced external force on the system.

Reveal solution

Total mass is $5.0\,\text{kg}$. External driving force is hanging weight: $F_g=(2)(10)=20\,\text{N}$. $a=20/5=4.0\,\text{m/s}^2$. For the table block, $T=ma=(3)(4)=12\,\text{N}$.

Level 13 • Connected Objects with Friction

Problem 13: Rough Table and Hanging Mass

A $6.0\,\text{kg}$ block on a horizontal table is connected by a light string over a pulley to a hanging $4.0\,\text{kg}$ mass. The coefficient of kinetic friction between the block and the table is $0.20$. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$.

  1. Identify the external forces on the two-block system along the direction of motion.
  2. Find the acceleration.
  3. Find the tension, then check it using the other block.
Reveal solution

Driving force is the hanging weight, $40\,\text{N}$. Friction on the table block is $(0.20)(6)(10)=12\,\text{N}$. $a=(40-12)/10=2.8\,\text{m/s}^2$. Hanging mass: $40-T=4(2.8)$, so $T=28.8\,\text{N}$. Check with the table block: $T-12=6(2.8)$ gives $T=28.8\,\text{N}$.

Level 12 • Atwood Machine

Problem 12: Two Hanging Masses

A $3.0\,\text{kg}$ mass and a $5.0\,\text{kg}$ mass hang on opposite sides of a light string over a light, frictionless pulley. They're released from rest. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$.

  1. Find the acceleration of the masses.
  2. Find the tension in the string.
  3. Is the tension greater than, less than, or equal to the weight of each mass? Explain why that makes sense.
Reveal scaffold

Treat both masses as one system. The net external force is the difference in weights, and the total mass is $8.0\,\text{kg}$. Then use one mass's FBD to find $T$.

Reveal solution

$a=\dfrac{(5-3)(10)}{8}=2.5\,\text{m/s}^2$. For the $3.0\,\text{kg}$ mass accelerating up: $T-30=3(2.5)$, so $T=37.5\,\text{N}$. Check with the $5.0\,\text{kg}$ mass: $50-T=5(2.5)$ gives $T=37.5\,\text{N}$. The tension sits between the two weights ($30\,\text{N}$ and $50\,\text{N}$). It must be more than $30\,\text{N}$ to accelerate the light mass up and less than $50\,\text{N}$ so the heavy mass accelerates down.

Level 14 • Incline + Pulley

Problem 14: Ramp Block and Hanging Mass

A $4.0\,\text{kg}$ block sits on a frictionless $30^\circ$ incline. A string tied to it runs over a pulley at the top of the incline to a hanging $3.0\,\text{kg}$ mass. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$.

  1. Which way does the system accelerate? Decide by comparing forces before calculating anything.
  2. Find the acceleration.
  3. Find the tension.
Reveal scaffold

Compare the hanging weight with the component of the ramp block's weight along the incline, $m_1g\sin\theta$. Whichever is bigger wins.

Reveal solution

Hanging weight is $30\,\text{N}$, and the ramp component is $(4)(10)(0.5)=20\,\text{N}$, so the hanging mass goes down and the block goes up the ramp. $a=(30-20)/7=1.4\,\text{m/s}^2$. $T=3(10-1.43)=25.7\,\text{N}$. Check: $T-20=4(1.43)$ gives $T=25.7\,\text{N}$.

Level 15 • Challenge: Balanced Forces in Motion

Problem 15: The Ramp That Doesn't Speed Up

A $5.0\,\text{kg}$ block on a $37^\circ$ incline ($\mu_k=0.25$) is connected over a pulley at the top to a hanging $2.0\,\text{kg}$ mass. A student gives the block a quick shove down the ramp and lets go. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$, $\sin37^\circ=0.60$, and $\cos37^\circ=0.80$.

  1. Once the block is sliding down the ramp, which way does friction act?
  2. Find the net force on the system while it slides.
  3. Describe the motion after the shove.
Reveal solution

The block slides down, so friction points up the ramp. Down-ramp force is $mg\sin37^\circ=30\,\text{N}$. Opposing it are the hanging weight, $20\,\text{N}$, and friction, $(0.25)(40)=10\,\text{N}$. Net force is $30-20-10=0$, so the system moves at constant velocity. This is Newton's 1st law: motion doesn't need a net force.

Level 16 • AP Qualitative Reasoning

Problem 16: Adding Mass to Both Sides

In the Atwood machine from Problem 12, a student adds $1.0\,\text{kg}$ to each side. Does the acceleration increase, decrease, or stay the same? Answer in a short paragraph using Newton's 2nd law for the system before doing any calculation, then confirm with numbers.

Reveal solution

It decreases. The net external force is the difference in weights, $(m_2-m_1)g$, which doesn't change because both sides gained the same mass. The total mass being accelerated goes up, so $a=\Sigma F/m_{total}$ gets smaller. Numbers: $a=20/10=2.0\,\text{m/s}^2$, down from $2.5\,\text{m/s}^2$.

Quick Check

AP Paragraph

Two masses hang on opposite sides of a light string over a frictionless pulley and accelerate after they are released. A student says the tension in the string equals the weight of the heavier mass.

Write a short paragraph that agrees or disagrees, using Newton's 2nd law for each mass.

Reveal strong response

Disagree. Tension isn't equal to the hanging weight when things accelerate. The heavy mass accelerates down, so the net force on it points down and its weight must be bigger than $T$. The light mass accelerates up, so $T$ must be bigger than its weight. The tension sits between the two weights.

Lesson 6 of 6

Gravity and Circular Motion

You should be able to…

  • Scale gravitational force with mass and distance.
  • Identify which real force provides the centripetal force.
  • Solve horizontal and vertical circles.
Big idea: Gravitational force depends on both masses and the distance between their centers. An object moving in a circle needs a net force toward the center, and a real force (tension, friction, the normal force, or gravity) has to provide it.
Common mistake: There is no centrifugal force. Nothing pushes an object outward; the net force points toward the center.

Key Equations: Gravity and Circular Motion

Universal Gravitation$$F_g=\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}$$Distance $r$ is measured from the planet's center, not the surface. Doubling $r$ divides the force by $2^2$.
Field Strength$$g=\frac{GM}{R^2}=\frac{F_g}{m}$$Surface gravity depends on both mass and the distance to the center.
Centripetal Acceleration$$a_c=\frac{v^2}{r}$$Points toward the center. A real force (tension, friction, normal, gravity) must provide $\Sigma F=\frac{mv^2}{r}$.

Commit to a prediction or a first step before you reveal a scaffold or solution. Problems go from easier to harder.

Level 20 • Universal Gravitation

Problem 20: Weight Far from Earth

An $80\,\text{kg}$ astronaut weighs $800\,\text{N}$ on Earth's surface. Earth's radius is $R_E$.

  1. What is the gravitational force on the astronaut at a distance $2R_E$ from Earth's center?
  2. What is the gravitational field strength there?
  3. Is the astronaut "weightless" there? Explain.
Reveal scaffold

$F_g=\dfrac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}$, so doubling $r$ divides the force by $2^2$. Distance is measured from Earth's center, not the surface.

Reveal solution

$F_g=800/4=200\,\text{N}$. $g=F_g/m=200/80=2.5\,\text{N/kg}$. The astronaut isn't weightless, since gravity is still $200\,\text{N}$. Feeling weightless in orbit happens because the astronaut and spacecraft are in free fall together, not because gravity is gone.

Level 21 • Gravitational Field Reasoning

Problem 21: A Bigger Planet

Planet X has twice Earth's mass and twice Earth's radius. Earth's surface field is $10\,\text{N/kg}$.

  1. Without plugging in $G$, find the gravitational field strength at Planet X's surface.
  2. A student says, "Planet X is more massive, so you'd weigh more there." Explain the student's error.
Reveal solution

$g=\dfrac{GM}{R^2}$. Doubling $M$ doubles $g$, and doubling $R$ divides it by 4, so $g_X=10\times\dfrac{2}{4}=5.0\,\text{N/kg}$. The student ignored the radius. Surface gravity depends on both mass and the distance to the center, and here the larger radius matters more.

Level 23 • Horizontal Circular Motion

Problem 23: Ball on a String

A $1.2\,\text{kg}$ puck on a frictionless table moves in a horizontal circle of radius $0.50\,\text{m}$, tied to a string fixed at the center. Its speed is $3.0\,\text{m/s}$.

  1. What force provides the centripetal force?
  2. Find the centripetal acceleration and the tension.
  3. The string breaks. Describe the puck's path.
Reveal solution

The string's tension provides it. $a_c=\dfrac{v^2}{r}=\dfrac{9.0}{0.50}=18\,\text{m/s}^2$ toward the center, and $T=ma_c=(1.2)(18)=21.6\,\text{N}$. Once the string breaks, no net horizontal force acts, so the puck moves in a straight line tangent to the circle at the point where it broke, not outward.

Level 24 • Friction as Centripetal Force

Problem 24: Car on a Flat Curve

A $1000\,\text{kg}$ car rounds a flat curve of radius $50\,\text{m}$. The coefficient of static friction between the tires and road is $0.80$. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$.

  1. Why is it static friction and not kinetic friction?
  2. Find the maximum speed the car can have without skidding.
  3. Would a heavier truck have a higher, lower, or the same maximum speed? Explain.
Reveal solution

The tires don't slide across the road sideways, so friction is static. $\mu_smg=\dfrac{mv^2}{r}$ gives $v=\sqrt{\mu_sgr}=\sqrt{(0.80)(10)(50)}=20\,\text{m/s}$. Mass cancels, so the truck has the same maximum speed (assuming the same tires and road).

Level 25 • Vertical Circular Motion

Problem 25: Over the Top of a Hill

A $1000\,\text{kg}$ car drives over the top of a hill shaped like a circular arc of radius $40\,\text{m}$. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$.

  1. Draw the FBD at the top of the hill. Which way is the acceleration?
  2. Find the normal force when the car's speed at the top is $10\,\text{m/s}$.
  3. At what speed would the normal force become zero?
Reveal scaffold

At the top, the center of the circle is below the car, so take down as positive: $mg-F_N=\dfrac{mv^2}{r}$.

Reveal solution

Weight points down and the normal force points up. The acceleration points down, toward the center. $F_N=m\left(g-\dfrac{v^2}{r}\right)=1000\left(10-\dfrac{100}{40}\right)=7500\,\text{N}$. Setting $F_N=0$ gives $v=\sqrt{gr}=\sqrt{400}=20\,\text{m/s}$. Above that speed the car leaves the road.

Level 26 • Vertical Circle, Bottom

Problem 26: Swinging Ball

A $0.20\,\text{kg}$ ball swings on a $0.80\,\text{m}$ string in a vertical circle. At the bottom it moves at $4.0\,\text{m/s}$. Use $g=10\,\text{m/s}^2$.

  1. Find the tension at the bottom.
  2. Is the tension greater than, less than, or equal to the ball's weight? Why?
Reveal solution

At the bottom the center is above, so $T-mg=\dfrac{mv^2}{r}$. $T=0.20\left(10+\dfrac{16}{0.80}\right)=0.20(30)=6.0\,\text{N}$. That's greater than the $2.0\,\text{N}$ weight, because the net force must point up toward the center.

Level 27 • AP Paragraph Response

Problem 27: The "Centrifugal Force" Misconception

As a car turns sharply left, a passenger slides toward the right door. The passenger says, "A centrifugal force pushed me outward." In a clear paragraph, explain what actually happens, using Newton's 1st and 2nd laws and naming the real force that eventually makes the passenger turn.

Reveal solution

No outward force acts on the passenger. By Newton's 1st law, the passenger tends to keep moving in a straight line while the car turns left underneath them, so relative to the car they seem to slide right. Once they reach the door, the door's normal force pushes them left, toward the center of the turn. By Newton's 2nd law, that inward net force provides the centripetal acceleration that makes them turn with the car.

Question 1: Constant Velocity

A box slides to the right at constant velocity. What must be true about the net force?

Explanation

Constant velocity means acceleration is zero. Since $\Sigma F = ma$, the net force must be zero.

Question 2: Normal Force

A box rests on a horizontal table with no vertical acceleration. Which statement is true?

Explanation

The normal force is a contact force. On a level surface with no other vertical forces and no vertical acceleration, $F_N=mg$.

Question 3: Action-Reaction Pair

A student pushes a wall. The wall pushes back on the student with...

Explanation

Newton's third law pairs are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, and act on different objects.
