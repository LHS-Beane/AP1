The Challenge

Design, build, and launch a water rocket made from 2.0 L plastic bottles that reaches a height of at least 45.0 m. Then act as a physicist: use what you measured during the flight to reconstruct the motion of your rocket and the forces acting on it.

This is a 150-point project that pulls together everything from Unit 1 (Kinematics) and Unit 2 (Force and Translational Dynamics). You will not be told which equation to use. Choosing the right model for each phase of the flight is part of the assignment.

Timeline

  • Thursday, October 15: Launch day. All data collection happens today, so make sure every measurement in the Data Collection tab has an owner.
  • Thursday, October 22: Complete report due. There is one week between launch and the due date and no checkpoint in between, so plan your time.

Success Criteria

  • Target Height: At least 45.0 m above the launch pad.
  • Safe, Stable Flight: The rocket must pass the pre-launch inspection and fly without breaking apart.
  • Evidence-Based Analysis: Every calculated quantity must be traceable to something your team measured, with units, uncertainty, and a clearly stated model.

Core Physics

The rocket flight has distinct phases. Before you start your analysis, decide for yourself what each phase looks like:

  1. Thrust phase: Pressurized air forces water out of the nozzle and the rocket accelerates upward.
  2. Coast phase: The water is gone and the rocket continues upward to its peak.
  3. Descent: The rocket falls back to the ground.

For each phase, you will need to describe the motion with kinematics and explain it with forces.

Construction & Materials

Allowed Materials

  • Two 2-liter plastic bottles (one for the pressure chamber, one for the nose cone or body extension).
  • Duct tape and masking tape.
  • Cardboard or foam board (fins, nose cone).
  • Ballast for balance (clay, coins, sand) if your design needs it.

Design Constraints

  1. The Engine: The bottle used as the pressure vessel must remain structurally unaltered (no cuts, holes, or heat treatment).
  2. Stability: Your rocket must include fins and a nose cone or other mass distribution that keeps it pointing upward.
  3. Water Volume: Your team chooses it. Record the exact volume you use and your reasoning for it.
  4. Launch Pressure: Do not exceed 115 psi. The launch pressure is set at the launch pad by the teacher.
  5. Documentation: You must record mass, dimensions, and the center of mass location of the finished rocket before launch day (see Data Collection).

Safety Rules

  • Safety glasses are required for everyone in the launch area.
  • Stay behind the launch line until the teacher declares the pad clear. Never walk toward a pressurized rocket.
  • Only the teacher operates the pump and the launch release.
  • A rocket with damaged bottles, loose fins, or visible creases in the pressure vessel will not be launched.

What You Need to Measure

Plan before launch day. A missing measurement cannot be recovered after the rocket is in the air. Assign jobs to every member of your team.

Before Launch

  • Mass of the empty rocket and mass of the rocket with water loaded (use the lab balance).
  • Volume of water loaded and the nozzle (opening) diameter.
  • Location of the center of mass of the empty rocket and of the loaded rocket, measured along the rocket from a defined reference point. Include a labeled sketch.
  • Mass and location (along the same axis) of each major component (nose cone, bottle body, fins, tape/ballast) so you can analyze the system's center of mass.

During Launch

  • Hang time: At least three team members time the entire flight independently with stopwatches. Record all values.
  • Slow-motion video: Record the launch at the highest frame rate your device allows, with a reference object of known length in frame. Use it to determine how long the thrust phase lasts.
  • Landing location: Measure the distance and compass direction (or angle relative to a reference line) from the launch pad to where the rocket lands.

AP Report Structure

Your report documents the engineering process and the physical analysis of the flight. It should be written so that another physics student could repeat your work from your report alone.

Required Sections

  1. Title Page: Student name, partner name(s), date, and title.
  2. Introduction: State the objective. Explain the physics models you will use (what is assumed about each phase of the flight). Include a prediction for your rocket's peak height with your reasoning.
  3. Procedure & Design: Labeled diagram of your rocket, a materials list, an explanation of your design choices (water volume, fin shape, mass distribution) and the steps you used to collect data safely.
  4. Data & Graphs: Trial data table(s), mean values with uncertainty, and the required graph (see Analysis Requirements).
  5. Calculations: Every quantity in the Analysis Requirements tab, with a symbolic starting equation, substitution with units, and a final answer with units and appropriate significant figures. Show a free-body diagram where required.
  6. Results & Discussion: A claim-evidence-reasoning (CER) analysis, a quantified error analysis, and a design improvement justified by your calculations.

Suggested Work Order

Several sections only need information you can gather before launch day. Finish them early so the week after the launch is reserved for the flight analysis.

  • Before October 15: Title page, Introduction (including your height prediction), Procedure & Design, and the center of mass calculation (B2) using your pre-launch measurements.
  • After launch: Data tables and uncertainty, Parts A and B (the remaining calculations), the velocity–time graph, and the Results & Discussion.

Expectations for Your Work

  • Define every variable and state the reference frame (which direction is positive, where y = 0).
  • Every calculation starts from a general equation, then shows substitution with units, then the answer. Do not skip algebra steps.
  • Where an assumption is made (for example, treating air resistance as negligible), state it and later check whether your data support it.
  • Graphs must have titles, labeled axes with units, scale, and clearly identified key points.

Part C — Required Graph Unit 1

The graph is scored in the Data & Graphs category of the rubric.

  • A velocity–time graph for the whole flight (launch to landing), built from your calculated values, with the thrust, coast, and descent phases labeled and key values marked.
  • State what the slope and the area under the curve represent physically, and verify at least one area against a displacement you calculated in Part A.

Uncertainty

For hang time, report the mean of all timers and trials and a measure of its uncertainty (such as range or standard deviation). Carry that uncertainty through to the maximum height (A1) by recalculating with the largest and smallest reasonable hang times. This feeds into your error analysis.

Discussion Question: Air Resistance

Your calculations assumed something about air resistance. Using your data and your free-body diagrams, argue whether that assumption was reasonable, and explain which of your results it would affect most and in which direction (too high or too low).
